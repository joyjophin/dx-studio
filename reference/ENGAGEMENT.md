# Craft for attention and understanding

Engagement is a hypothesis about an audience. No hook or animation guarantees views.
Build a useful story, then test whether real viewers follow it.

## The brief that guides decisions

Capture: audience and prior knowledge; one question to answer; the concrete takeaway;
format and length; tone; source material; desired action. Prefer a narrower promise
with convincing evidence over several loosely connected tips.

## Story patterns

| Pattern | Hook | Middle | Payoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Problem → mechanism → fix | Show the failure | Reveal why it happens | Demonstrate the corrected result |
| Before → after → how | Show the transformation | Reconstruct the useful steps | Let viewers apply one rule |
| Misconception → evidence → model | State the tempting belief | Test it fairly | Offer a better explanation |
| Question → experiment → answer | Pose a specific question | Make the outcome visible | Resolve the exact opening question |
| Tutorial | Show the finished result | Teach only the necessary steps | Replay the result and the key shortcut |

Generate three **meaningfully different** hook candidates internally when the brief
needs one. Choose the clearest promise for this audience. Avoid introductions, vague
teasers, artificial urgency and delaying an answer just to extend runtime.

## Beat sheet

For each beat record: time window, spoken line, visual proof, what changes, caption
phrase, sound cue, selectable layer IDs. Reveal the visual when the narration names
it. A diagram should explain a relationship; a counter needs a real value and source.

A 30–60-second video often benefits from a useful visual change every 2–4 seconds;
that is a starting heuristic, not a timer. Hold a complex demonstration longer.
Cut when the thought changes. Silence and stillness can supply contrast. A new zoom
is not new information. Use an open question only if its answer genuinely follows.

## Screen and audio

- Prioritise one subject per shot, readable at phone size. Stage context, zoom to the
  relevant detail, demonstrate, then show the result in context.
- Avoid repeating a full voice sentence on screen. Captions provide access; labels,
  numbers, comparisons and diagrams provide evidence.
- Caption phrases should follow speech and syntax. Start around 3–7 words; allow time
  to read them. Highlight the meaningful word instead of every word.
- Move with purpose: indicate cause/effect, reveal hierarchy, connect two ideas.
  Land camera moves before the action. Avoid constant shake, flash or drifting pans.
- Keep music below narration. Use SFX on meaningful changes, and leave some moments
  quiet. Check the final master rather than assuming the loudness target was met.

## Endings

Resolve the opening promise before asking for anything. A useful closing action is
specific to the lesson: try the technique, compare an example, or keep a reference.
Loop only when it adds meaning and does not hide a missing conclusion.

## Editorial review

Check the first three seconds for topic clarity, every beat for new information,
the longest text frame for reading time, and the ending for a complete answer.
Score clarity, evidence, pacing, readability and editability from 1–5; use the scores
to identify revision needs, never as measured audience retention.

When analytics are available, compare hook hold, average percentage watched, drop-off
points, replays and saves with the creator's own baseline. Change one major story
choice at a time. Use observed results to update the next brief.
